Limits

A limit tells us what value a function gets close to when \(x\) gets close to a number.

We will use tables, graphs and calculations to find limits.

Gap from \(x\) to 3
Gap from \(f(x)\) to the target
\[ \lim_{x \to 3} (2x + 1) = 7 \]

Read this as "the limit of \(2x + 1\) as \(x\) gets close to 3 is 7". Here \(f(3) = 7\) as well, so the limit and the value at the point are the same. That is not always true, as station 04 will show.

The notation

\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \]

As \(x\) gets close to \(a\), \(f(x)\) gets close to \(L\). The limit is about what happens near \(a\), not only at \(a\).

05

Does the limit exist?

70–80 min

A limit exists when both sides move towards the same number. Look from both sides, then decide.

More detail: \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x}\) near 0

From the left, \(\frac{1}{x}\) becomes a larger and larger negative number. From the right, it becomes a larger and larger positive number. We write \(f(x) \to -\infty\) and \(f(x) \to \infty\). Neither side settles on a number, and the two sides go opposite ways, so there is no finite two-sided limit. \(f(0)\) is not defined, because we cannot divide by 0.

06

Calculate limits using algebra

80–95 min

Try putting the number into the expression. If you get \(\frac{0}{0}\), simplify the expression first.

The method

  1. Substitute the value of \(x\). If you get a number, that is the limit.
  2. If you get \(\frac{0}{0}\), you cannot find the limit by direct substitution. For the questions in this lesson, try factorising and cancelling the common factor.
  3. Substitute into the simplified expression.
Why \(\frac{0}{0}\) is not the answer

\(\frac{0}{0}\) is not 0, not 1 and not a number at all. It only tells us the expression cannot be used as it stands at that point. Cancelling a factor such as \((x - 2)\) is allowed for every \(x \ne 2\). The limit only looks at values near 2, never at 2 itself, so the simplified expression gives the same limit.
