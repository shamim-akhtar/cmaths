1.What is the order of \( \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 1 & 7 \\ 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \)? Give rows first.
×
Count the rows from the top: there are 3. Count the columns from the left: there are 2. The order is \(3 \times 2\). Rows always come first.
2.Let \( P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \) and \( Q = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \). Find the entry in row 2, column 1 of \(PQ\).
Take row 2 of \(P\), which is \((3,\,0)\), and column 1 of \(Q\), which is \((2,\,1)\). Multiply the pairs and add: \( 3 \times 2 + 0 \times 1 = 6 \).
3.Let \( M = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 0 & 8 \end{pmatrix} \). Find \(IM\), where \(I\) is the \(2 \times 2\) identity matrix.
Multiplying by the identity matrix changes nothing, so \(IM = M\). Check one entry: row 1 of \(I\) is \((1,\,0)\), column 1 of \(M\) is \((5,\,0)\), and \(1 \times 5 + 0 \times 0 = 5\). This is the fact that makes the inverse work later in the lesson.

02

The transpose of a matrix

10–15 min

The transpose of a matrix changes its rows into columns. It is written \(A^{\mathsf{T}}\). Let's see how it works.

Watch the rows become columns

Change any of the six numbers, then press Play all, or One number at a time to move them yourself. Watch where each number lands.

A is 2 × 3

Row 1 becomes column 1. Row 2 becomes column 2. A matrix with 2 rows and 3 columns becomes a matrix with 3 rows and 2 columns.

More detail
\[ \bigl(A^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)_{ij} = a_{ji} \]

The entry in row \(i\), column \(j\) of the transpose is the entry in row \(j\), column \(i\) of the original. The row number and the column number swap places. The main diagonal stays where it is, and transposing twice gives back the original matrix.

Two predictions

1.A matrix \(B\) has order \(3 \times 4\). What is the order of \(B^{\mathsf{T}}\)?
×
Rows become columns, so the 3 rows of \(B\) become the 3 columns of \(B^{\mathsf{T}}\). The order is \(4 \times 3\).
2.Let \( C = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 9 & 4 \\ 6 & 1 & 8 \end{pmatrix} \). Without writing out \(C^{\mathsf{T}}\), what is the entry in row 2, column 1 of \(C^{\mathsf{T}}\)?
Swap the row and column numbers. Row 2, column 1 of \(C^{\mathsf{T}}\) is row 1, column 2 of \(C\), which is 9.

03

What is a determinant?

15–23 min

First, a new word. The inverse of a matrix is another matrix that reverses what it does. For example, if a matrix makes a shape twice as big, its inverse makes it half the size again. You will learn how to find an inverse in station 04.

Not every matrix has an inverse. The determinant tells us which ones do. It is a number that we calculate from a square matrix.

  • If the determinant is 0, the matrix has no inverse.
  • If the determinant is not 0, the matrix has an inverse.

Let's learn how to calculate it.

How to calculate the determinant

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \]
\[ \det(A) = ad - bc \]

Multiply the main diagonal, top left to bottom right. Multiply the other diagonal, top right to bottom left. Subtract the second from the first.

More detail: positive and negative

The size of the determinant is the area factor. The sign tells you the direction. A positive determinant keeps the shape the same way round. A negative determinant turns it over, like turning a page. In the figure, the blue arrow going anticlockwise to the orange arrow means positive; clockwise means negative. Both have an inverse. Only 0 does not.

04

Finding the inverse of a 2×2 matrix

23–30 min

An inverse is a matrix that reverses what another matrix does. It is written \(A^{-1}\). Let's learn how to find it.

The three steps

  1. Calculate the determinant. If it is 0, stop: there is no inverse.
  2. Find the adjoint. Swap the two entries on the main diagonal, and change the sign of the other two.
  3. Divide the adjoint by the determinant.
\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \]
\[ \operatorname{adj}(A) = \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} \]
\[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)}\,\operatorname{adj}(A) \]
More detail

Multiplying a matrix by its inverse gives the identity matrix: \( A\,A^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I \). This is how you check an inverse. The adjoint is sometimes called the adjugate. It is not the same as the transpose.

The determinant of a 3×3 matrix

30–40 min

A \(3 \times 3\) determinant is built from three \(2 \times 2\) determinants.

Cross out the row and column of an entry. Four numbers remain. Calculate the determinant of this smaller \(2 \times 2\) matrix. This number is called the minor.

Do this for each entry in the first row. Multiply each entry by its minor, use the signs \(+\), \(-\), \(+\), and add.

\[ \det\begin{pmatrix} a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i \end{pmatrix} = a\begin{vmatrix} e & f \\ h & i \end{vmatrix} - b\begin{vmatrix} d & f \\ g & i \end{vmatrix} + c\begin{vmatrix} d & e \\ g & h \end{vmatrix} \]

06

The inverse of a 3×3 matrix

40–50 min

Finding a \(3 \times 3\) inverse takes more steps. We will work through them one at a time.

The four steps

  1. Calculate the determinant, as in station 05. If it is 0, stop: there is no inverse.
  2. Find the cofactor of every entry. There are nine. Cross out the entry's row and column, find the minor, then give it a sign from the pattern below.
  3. Transpose the matrix of cofactors. This is the adjoint.
  4. Divide the adjoint by the determinant.
\[ \text{Sign pattern:}\quad \begin{pmatrix} + & - & + \\ - & + & - \\ + & - & + \end{pmatrix} \]
\[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)}\,\operatorname{adj}(A) \]

09

Undoing a transformation in a game

105–113 minOptional application

In games, matrices can change the size, position or direction of objects. An inverse matrix can help reverse these changes.

The sprite in its own coordinates

Where else this is used

Optional reading: 3×3 matrices in graphics

A \(3 \times 3\) matrix can mean two different things in graphics. It can be a transformation of 3D space. It can also be a transformation of 2D points written with a third coordinate equal to 1, which lets a single matrix include a move. Both types of matrices have inverses if their determinants are not zero.
